Benchmark Metrics

Nine models for Austrian imbalance-price forecasting, measured under identical leak-free conditions.

Market APG control area (AT)  ·  Data 140,249 quarter-hours, 2022–2025  ·  Test 2025, 4 rolling folds, 34,521 origins × 96 horizons  ·  Seed 42  ·  Date 31 Aug 2026

1. Conditions

Every model was trained on identical rolling quarterly folds with identical scaling, identical known-future covariates, identical day-ahead outage dropout, and the day-ahead gate-closure fix enabled — so no model sees an auction price that had not yet cleared at its forecast origin. The design is a 2 × 3 grid (market-rule blocks against a plain mixer, across Dense, LSTM and GRU encoders) plus four references. Reading down a column isolates the encoder; reading across a row isolates the market rules.

All accuracy figures are pooled on concatenated per-fold predictions, each already inverted to EUR/MWh inside its own fold. This matters: RMSE is a root of a mean of squares and does not average across folds — the mean of four fold-RMSEs understates the pooled value by roughly 17%.

3. What each column means

MetricUnits / rangeDefinition and why it is here
AQLEUR/MWh, lower betterAverage quantile loss — mean pinball loss over the five quantiles (0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9). This is the headline metric because the model is probabilistic: it scores the whole predicted distribution, not just the central estimate.
MAEEUR/MWh, lower betterMean absolute error of the median (q50) forecast. Interpretable in market units and robust to the extreme tail.
RMSEEUR/MWh, lower betterRoot mean squared error of q50. Squaring makes it dominated by spikes — the imbalance price reaches ±15,000 EUR/MWh — which is why all models sit near 390 regardless of skill.
sMAPE%, 0–200, lower betterSymmetric mean absolute percentage error. Included on request; see the caveat below.
MASEratio, lower betterMean absolute scaled error, scaled by the seasonal-naive change at season = 96. Scale-free and, unlike sMAPE, valid with negative prices. Below 1 means better than seasonal naive — every model qualifies.
AQCR%, lower betterAverage quantile crossing rate — how often predicted quantiles cross (e.g. q75 below q50), which is logically impossible and signals a broken forecast distribution.
ParamscountTrainable weights. A proxy for model capacity and overfitting risk.
vs clim%, negative betterImprovement over climatology — predicting the training-set quantiles for every interval. This is the honest reference: beating a seasonal naive on this series is easy, beating climatology is not.
Train / Val / Testseconds per foldComputational cost split into fitting, per-epoch validation, and inference over the test set.
Caveat on sMAPE. It is computable here — the denominator |y| + |ŷ| never vanishes — but it saturates. 6.8% of target cells are negative, and 8.8% sit at the 200% ceiling, where the metric returns the same value whether the miss is 100 or 10,000 EUR/MWh. Those cells are 9.2% of the data but 29.6% of the metric, and their mean absolute error is 174 EUR/MWh. sMAPE therefore cannot rank tail behaviour, which is what this market turns on. It ranks the models in the same order as AQL, so it is not misleading as an ordering — but its magnitude should not be interpreted. MASE is reported beside it for that reason.
Note on timing. Train and validation are the measured per-epoch cost multiplied by the recorded epoch count. Per-epoch cost is taken as a slope — differencing a 6-epoch fit against a 2-epoch fit — so the fixed graph-tracing and setup overhead cancels rather than being multiplied by the epoch count. Test time and data preparation are measured directly. Reconstructed totals land 8–12% below the originally recorded per-fold wall time, which is expected: that figure also covered inverse-transforming, per-horizon scoring and writing prediction archives. XGBoost has no epochs, so its training figure is a direct measurement of the full 96-booster fit and validation does not apply; its Params is blank because the stored value was 2,993 boosting rounds, not weights, and printing that beside a neural weight count would invite a false comparison.

6. How to read the table

MR-GRU is the best model, and it is also the smallest

It wins every accuracy column while using 5,055 parameters — the fewest of any neural model, a third of AttnBiLSTM's and a hundredth of LQR's — and completes the four-fold study in 49 minutes against AttnBiLSTM's 57 and XGBoost's 187. That is a clean accuracy-per-parameter and accuracy-per-second argument. Inference is under one second per fold for all nine models, so the rule blocks impose no deployment cost, only training cost.

The encoder swap is real but small

MR-GRU beats MRINN by 0.245 AQL, significant on both tests — a genuine effect, but a 0.95% improvement. Changing the encoder alone is a thin contribution.

The market rules are worth about 0.53% — and only at longer horizons

Averaged across the three encoders the rule blocks buy roughly half a percent, and only the GRU pairing reaches significance. The per-horizon table shows why the pooled figure misleads: at 15 minutes the rules actively hurt both recurrent models (plain GRU 24.57 against MR-GRU 24.92; plain LSTM 24.85 against MR-LSTM 25.18), and the sign only flips by day-ahead. The defensible claim is therefore narrower and more interesting than "market rules help": rule structure costs accuracy where persistence is strong, and earns its place as the horizon lengthens.

XGBoost is the mirror image

It is the strongest model at 15 minutes by a wide margin (AQL 22.97, 8% clear of MR-GRU) and second-worst at a day (26.88). Its advantage is entirely short-horizon, which is the same effect seen from the other side.

Only two models can produce an invalid distribution

AQCR is exactly zero for seven of nine. That is earned by the hierarchical quantile head, which makes crossing structurally impossible, not by the encoder. XGBoost (2.75%) and LQR (18.07%) lack that head, and LQR's figure means nearly one prediction in five is internally inconsistent.

The honest headline. The best model improves on climatology by 12.3% AQL and 13.6% MAE. Against the seasonal naive the same model looks far better, but that baseline is weak on this series and should not be used for the headline. All nine models sit within 3% of one another, which is itself a finding: the residual error is dominated by the unpredictability of the imbalance direction, not by model choice.
